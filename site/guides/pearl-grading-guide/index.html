Pearl grading for wholesale buyers: the five factors to put in your spec

Tianqi Jewelry · Shanxiahu, Zhuji · Updated 13 November 2026

Two lots labelled "AAA" from two suppliers can look very different. There is no single international letter scale for pearls: A–AAA (or A–D) is trade shorthand, and each supplier sets its own line between the letters.

Laboratories and national standards grade separate quality factors instead. GIA uses seven value factors, and China's national standard GB/T 18781-2023 Pearl grading covers colour, size, shape, lustre, surface, nacre thickness and matching. For a buyer the practical answer is the same: describe each factor in your request for quote, and approve a sample before bulk.

This guide explains the five factors we quote against at our sourcing base in Shanxiahu, Zhuji — lustre, surface, shape, size and matching — plus colour and nacre, and shows how to write them into a spec.

Lustre

Lustre is the light a pearl reflects from its surface and from just below it. High-lustre pearls show a bright, sharp reflection, almost like a mirror; low-lustre pearls look soft or chalky. Many buyers see lustre as the factor that makes a pearl look "alive", and it is the first thing end customers notice.

Lustre is hard to judge from retouched or over-lit photos. Ask for pictures in daylight or under a plain white light, with a recognisable object reflected on the pearls.

Surface

Pearls grow in a living animal, so almost every pearl has some marks: small spots, pits, wrinkles, growth lines or bumps. Surface grading is about how many there are, how large and how visible they are, and where they sit. A mark next to the drill hole or under a setting matters far less than one on the face of a stud earring.

Tell your supplier how the pearls will be used. For studs, the visible face must be clean; for strands, marks spread around the pearl are more noticeable; for set pendants, one clean side may be enough.

Shape

Shapes run from round and near-round through oval, drop and button to baroque (free-form). Perfectly round pearls are the rarest, so they cost the most. Drop and button shapes suit earrings and pendants, and baroque pearls are popular in contemporary designs because every piece is different.

For round pearls, say whether near-round is acceptable. For baroque, describe the look you want and approve a sample, because a single word covers a very wide range.

Size

Size is the diameter in millimetres, quoted in 0.5 mm steps (for example 7.0–7.5 mm). Strands are graduated or uniform: a graduated strand lists end-to-centre sizes, such as 6–9 mm. Larger pearls take longer to grow and cost more, often sharply more at the top of each pearl type's range.

Typical ranges: freshwater 2–12 mm, Akoya 2–10 mm, Tahitian 8–16 mm and South Sea 9–20 mm.

Matching

Matching is how consistent pearls are when they sit together: colour, overtone, lustre, size and shape across a pair of earrings or a strand. It is what makes a strand look like one piece rather than a mix. Close matching takes time and a larger pool of pearls to choose from, which is one reason sourcing at the origin helps.

If you sell pairs or strands, put matching in the spec explicitly. Otherwise each pearl may be in range on its own and still not look right next to its partner.

Colour and nacre

Colour combines body colour (white, pink, lavender, gold, dark), overtone (the tint on top, such as pink or silver) and, on some pearls, orient — a shimmer of colours. Specify the body colour and overtone you want, and whether the colour must be natural. We state the pearl type and any treatment on the invoice.

Nacre is the layered material that makes up the pearl. Akoya, South Sea, Tahitian and Edison pearls grow around a bead, so the thickness of the nacre over the bead matters for durability and lustre. Most freshwater pearls, and keshi pearls, form without a bead and are almost entirely nacre.

What to do with letter grades

Letter grades are fine as a shorthand once you and your supplier agree on what they mean. Before you rely on them:

  • Ask the supplier to describe each letter in terms of the five factors above.
  • When you compare suppliers, compare samples or photos taken under the same light, not the letters.
  • Keep the approved sample. It is the reference for every later order.

How to write a pearl spec

A good request for quote states each factor, the quantity and how the pearls will be used. For example:

Pearl type: cultured freshwater, natural colour Shape: round (near-round acceptable) Size: 7.0–7.5 mm Colour: white body, pink overtone Lustre: high — sharp reflection Surface: clean to the eye on the face; minor marks near the drill hole accepted Matching: matched in pairs (colour, lustre, size) Drilling: half-drilled, for stud earrings Quantity: 200 pairs Delivery: Los Angeles, USA (DDP)

Not sure about one of the lines? Leave it open and tell us how the pearls will be used; we will suggest what fits your price range and show you photos.

Questions buyers ask

Is there an official pearl grading standard?

There is no single international letter scale. Laboratories and national standards grade separate quality factors instead: GIA uses seven value factors, and China's national standard GB/T 18781-2023 "Pearl grading" covers colour, size, shape, lustre, surface, nacre thickness and matching. Letter grades such as AAA are trade shorthand that each supplier defines for itself.

What does AAA mean when a supplier uses it?

Whatever that supplier says it means. One supplier's AAA can be another's AA. Ask the supplier to describe each grade in terms of lustre, surface, shape, size and matching, and compare samples photographed under the same light.

Which factors move the price most?

Size and lustre usually have the largest effect, followed by surface and shape. Close matching adds cost too, because more pearls have to be sorted to build each pair or strand.

How do I check quality before I pay?

Ask for photos and a short video of the actual lot, in daylight or under a plain white light, with the pearls rolled so you see the whole surface. For wholesale and custom orders, approve a paid sample first and have the bulk order checked against it.

Can I get a third-party test report?

Yes, on request. Tell us when you send your inquiry and we will arrange it with the order details the laboratory needs.
